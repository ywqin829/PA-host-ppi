## Title

PA-HIR: a bias-audited Pseudomonas aeruginosa host-interaction resource for context-aware analysis of infection transcriptomes

## ABSTRACT

Curated host–pathogen interaction maps are sparse, strongly influenced by study intensity, and usually separated from the infection-response data needed to interpret direction. We developed the Pseudomonas aeruginosa Host Interaction Resource (PA-HIR), a bias-audited resource that keeps interaction evidence, cross-pathogen context and infection-response data as separate, queryable layers. PA-HIR contains 132 curated PA–human pairs, a 10,491-pair cross-pathogen interactome spanning 102 bacterial taxa, and eleven public infection transcriptomic resources, with source, assay class, replication unit, effect direction and uncertainty retained for each layer. We demonstrate its use in five analyses. PA-target enrichment among recurrent host hubs depended on curation-source composition. An ESM2 classifier fell from AUC 0.78 against random negatives to 0.41 against high-degree host-negative pools and 0.25 against the highest-degree stratum, demonstrating that apparent predictive performance was highly sensitive to negative-set composition and host-node degree, leaving pathogen-specific interaction discrimination unresolved. Across nine eligible datasets, the pooled TLR5 effect was negative (−0.86, 95% CI −1.50 to −0.22), but heterogeneity was very high and the prediction interval crossed zero; exclusion of the two reanalysed SRP-linked datasets rendered the confidence interval compatible with no average effect. In a six-donor primary human bronchial epithelial dataset, TLR5 decreased in all 24 condition pairs across four arsenic doses; donor-level inference confirmed the decrease while NF-κB-associated and inflammatory-response transcripts increased, demonstrating divergence between TLR5 transcript abundance and the broader inflammatory transcriptional response. Cross-context comparisons showed that transcripts encoding the previously linked proteins TLR5 and MUC1 did not exhibit a fixed pattern of co-regulation across infection models: acute primary epithelium was predominantly down, persistent Calu-3 infection was up and iPSC macrophages were TLR5-down/MUC1-up. PA-HIR therefore shifts host-directed candidate assessment from a scalar ranking to an auditable set of interaction, expression and context layers and provides a reusable framework for testing host-directed hypotheses in other pathogens.

## IMPACT STATEMENT

Host-directed studies of Pseudomonas aeruginosa need to distinguish genuine recurrent interactions from study intensity, network-hub bias and infection-context effects. Most resources return a ranked target list without keeping those sources of evidence separate. PA-HIR integrates curated interaction evidence, cross-pathogen context and infection transcriptomes in one auditable resource, quantifies the bias in each layer and preserves conflicting directions instead of forcing them into one score. The benchmark also challenges the common use of random negatives for interaction classifiers: degree-matched negative pools remove much of the apparent predictive signal. Its outputs support reproducible queries for individual genes, pathogens or evidence classes and show where a candidate is supported, context-bound or unresolved. The resource provides a practical basis for evaluating host-directed hypotheses while keeping ranking, biological interpretation and intervention claims separate.

KEYWORDS: Pseudomonas aeruginosa; host-pathogen interaction; evidence resource; transcriptomics; reproducibility; bias assessment; meta-analysis

## DATA SUMMARY

This study reanalysed eleven public P. aeruginosa infection transcriptomic resources available under GEO/SRA accessions GSE192528, GSE201465, GSE262433, GSE274823, GSE276816, GSE287469, GSE324794, GSE334176, GSE97036, SRP353174 (GSE192890) and SRP439193 (GSE233206), together with the published interaction resources cited in the text. No new human or animal data were generated. Processed tables and model outputs are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S23, and the versioned analysis inputs, scripts and result tables are available at https://github.com/ywqin829/PA-host-ppi in release `v9.0.0`, under data/v9 and scripts/v9, and permanently archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23667995. The underlying source data remain subject to the terms of their original repositories.

## INTRODUCTION

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) is a leading cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), chronic airway infection in cystic fibrosis (CF) and invasive disease in immunocompromised hosts (1). Its pathogenesis depends on secreted effectors, adhesins and proteases that engage host receptors, signalling scaffolds and cytoskeletal regulators (2, 3). Curated PA–human interaction resources nevertheless contain only about 130 experimentally or functionally supported protein pairs (4), far fewer than the host processes altered during infection, and those pairs are concentrated on proteins that have been studied repeatedly.

Cross-species interaction data can increase coverage, because unrelated bacterial pathogens repeatedly engage overlapping sets of host proteins (5, 6). Apparent convergence, however, can arise from unequal study intensity, differences in assay detectability, and the repeated recovery of promiscuous hubs (7). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks carry this ascertainment bias structurally, and sequence-embedding interaction classifiers inherit it whenever random negatives are less hub-like than true partners (8). Hub ranking on its own therefore cannot distinguish biological convergence from detection intensity.

Direction is a second and independent gap. Network centrality carries no information about whether a host factor is induced or suppressed during infection, whether its function is protective or injurious, or which therapeutic direction - enhancement, restoration or inhibition - would be appropriate. Because host-directed candidates for VAP and CF are entering development, a ranking used without direction information can nominate a mechanistically wrong or harmful intervention.

We therefore developed PA-HIR, a versioned, bias-audited resource for PA host-interaction evidence. The resource keeps five outputs separate: the curated PA–human interaction map; the cross-pathogen context in which host proteins recur; infection-response estimates across tissues and model systems; the source dependence and degree dependence of computational enrichment; and a record of conflicting or context-bound evidence. This separation allows a user to ask whether a host candidate has interaction support, whether that support survives known network biases, what direction of change has been measured, and whether the direction is stable across infection contexts. TLR5 is used as an application of the resource rather than as a claim of a previously unknown interaction, because flagellin-TLR5 signalling is already established in PA infection. MUC1 has been reported to associate with TLR5 and inhibit MyD88 recruitment (37). The application therefore asks whether transcripts encoding these previously linked proteins vary together across infection contexts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 Data sources and curation

UniProt reviewed proteomes (release 2024; (9)) were used for PAO1 (5,583 proteins; taxid 208964) and human (20,432 proteins). PA–human records were assembled from a 2025 published PA-human interaction resource (4), PHI-base v5.6 (10), IntAct (5), the Host-Pathogen Interaction Database (HPIDB; (11); unavailable at the recorded access time, with overlapping IMEx records recovered through IntAct) and literature on established effectors or adhesins (12, 13). Accessions were validated against both proteomes and deduplicated by PA–human pair. S1 records the source and classifies evidence as physical interaction or functional/database association. The full 132-pair set was the primary coverage resource; the 49 physical pairs were used as a restriction analysis.

2.2 Cross-species interactome and convergence

The full IntAct Molecular Interaction Tab (MITAB) 2.7 release (accessed 2026-08-15) was parsed with multi-token taxid handling and species-name matching; 10,491 non-PA bacteria–human pairs from 102 taxa were retained. Cross-species degree was the number of distinct bacterial taxa linked to each human protein. Because species–human edges were unique, the reported co-targeting count is the same statistic as bipartite degree.

PA-target enrichment was evaluated with a Mann–Whitney test and a Fisher test comparing the two highest preassigned rank deciles with the remaining deciles. Interaction-count-stratified permutation preserved the PA-target distribution across deciles of bacterial interaction-record count: in each of 10,000 iterations, non-PA proteins were sampled within each stratum, with replacement only where a stratum lacked sufficient candidates. The one-sided empirical p value used (b+1)/(B+1). Sensitivity analyses restricted PA evidence to physical interactions and removed one source at a time. A separate multivariable logistic regression was fitted in the 461 proteins with complete cached PubMed-count and local UniProt-length covariates: PA-target status ~ cross-species degree + log10(1+PubMed count) + log2(protein length). The model was repeated after excluding the fixed pattern-recognition receptor (PRR)/adaptor panel. These regressions are sensitivity analyses.

2.3 Embedding classifier and benchmark design

Proteins were embedded with ESM2-650M (Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2; (14); layer 33, mean pooling, 1,280 dimensions, fp16); pair features were reduced (TruncatedSVD, 200 components), scaled and classified with logistic regression or a multilayer perceptron (MLP). Protein-disjoint five-fold cross-validation (CV), transfer to the PA test set with bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) and five negative-set resamplings, leave-one-pathogen-out, and three hard-negative designs (random; the top-300 high-degree host proteins without PA annotation; and cross-species partner-like proteins) with dedicated deterministic seeds, plus a degree-stratified series, evaluated the association between negative-pool host degree and transfer AUC by Spearman correlation; the coefficient is reported in Results. The classifier is used as a candidate coverage screen; the biological claims rest on the curated interactome and infection-response data.

2.4 Infection-expression datasets and statistical models

Public PA infection-expression datasets were identified from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)/Sequence Read Archive (SRA) records and associated literature available to the study team through 2026-08-17. This was a curated, non-systematic collection rather than a registered systematic review. Eligibility for formal TLR5 meta-analysis required a PA-vs-uninfected comparison, quantifiable TLR5 effect and uncertainty, and biological replication at the analyzed dataset level. When a dataset contributed multiple cell types, effects were collapsed to one dataset-level estimate for the primary model. Single-donor technical replicates and pooled single-cell libraries were Tier 3 evidence and were excluded from formal meta-analysis. Table 1 and the dataset-independence matrix record the analyzed contrast and replication unit.

GSE192528 (human lung explants, five donors × PA/influenza A virus (IAV)/bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG)/uninfected; (15)) was analyzed by donor-blocked DESeq2 (~ donor + pathogen) with prespecified PA-vs-control, PA-vs-IAV and PA-vs-BCG contrasts and genome-wide Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction. The PA-preference statistic was S = ΔPA − mean(ΔIAV, ΔBCG). GSE334176 (HCE-2 corneal epithelium, PA vs uninfected control, n=3 per group) was analyzed with DESeq2. GSE324794 (severe-pneumonia acute-kidney-injury [SP-AKI] mouse lung, n=5 per group; (16)) provided FPKM values transformed as log2(FPKM+1). The TLR5 log2FC was the difference in group means (SP-AKI minus control), its standard error was the Welch two-sample t-test standard error, and its nominal p value was the two-sided Welch two-sample t-test p value. GSE287469 (ER-Hoxb8 monocytic [M-] and polymorphonuclear [PMN-] myeloid-derived suppressor cells [MDSCs], control/PAO1 wild type [WT]/ΔFliC, n=3 per group; (17)) was analyzed with limma-voom using a cell-type × treatment design, within-cell-type contrasts and two one-sided test (TOST) equivalence bounds of ±0.5 and ±1.0 log2FC. GSE97036 (six donor primary human bronchial epithelial [HBE] cultures exposed to 0-50 µg/L arsenic and PAO1 for 6 h; (36)) was analyzed with a negative-binomial generalized linear mixed model: count ~ infection × arsenic + offset(log size factor) + (1 | donor). Donor was a random intercept; the fixed-effect matrix for infection × arsenic contained eight columns and was full rank. A separate donor-fixed negative-binomial sensitivity model, count ~ donor + infection × arsenic + offset(log size factor), contained 13 fixed-effect columns and was full rank. Arsenic-by-infection interaction was tested by likelihood-ratio comparison with the reduced random-intercept model count ~ infection + arsenic + offset(log size factor) + (1 | donor), giving 3 df. For the primary biological analysis, each donor–arsenic pair was summarized as Δlog2FC = log2(normalized PA count + 1) − log2(normalized control count + 1), where normalized counts used the common DESeq2 size factors and a pseudocount of 1 prevented undefined ratios. The four arsenic-level Δlog2FC values were averaged within each donor; the mean, standard error (SD/√6), 95% confidence interval and two-sided one-sample t test were then calculated across the six donor values. This six-donor normalized-count log2FC and SE were used in the cross-study meta-analysis. Model and donor-level outputs are provided in Supplementary Tables S22a-S22g. GSE276816 (one induced pluripotent stem cell [iPSC] donor per genotype with duplicate RNA; (18)) and GSE274823 (bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [BALF] from three mice pooled into one library per condition; (19)) were descriptive datasets. Mouse Ensembl identifiers were mapped to Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) symbols.

For GSE262433 Calu-3 dual RNA-seq (20), STAR unstranded ReadsPerGene host counts were filtered to genes with at least 10 counts in at least two samples and analyzed by DESeq2 with a four-level time/condition group design. PA-vs-control contrasts were estimated separately at 24 h (control n=2, PA n=3) and 120 h (n=3 per group), with BH correction. The 120-h contrast was used as the dataset-level persistent-infection endpoint because it is balanced and represents sustained infection; the 24-h contrast was analyzed as a sensitivity endpoint. GSE262433 was included in the primary model, with exclusion of persistent infection prespecified as a sensitivity analysis. PAO1 featureCounts tables were analyzed separately with a three-level bacterial-library model (24 h, 120 h and planktonic medium); the reported bacterial contrast was 120 h vs 24 h. Normalized counts, model log2FC, standard error (SE), p and adjusted p are released for both organisms. Genes with Cook’s-distance outlier filtering and no adjusted p were reported without adjusted values.

2.5 Single-cell analysis

GSE274823 (mouse BALF, WT/double knockout (DKO) × sham/PA; 10x Genomics; (19)) was processed with scanpy (quality control [QC] ≥200 genes/cell and <20% mitochondrial reads), yielding 24,614 cells. Leiden clusters were annotated by marker scoring. Tlr5 was summarized by cell type as mean log-normalized expression and detected fraction. Because each condition was represented by one pooled library, infection and library effects are not separable. We therefore report only descriptive within-library localization and do not use cells as independent biological replicates or present inferential infection effects.

2.6 Cross-study meta-analysis

Study-level TLR5 log2FC and SE were taken from uniform reanalyses of the deposited data. For SRP439193/GSE233206, we fitted DESeq2 to the available mouse lung raw counts (n=3 per group). For SRP353174/GSE192890, we fitted limma to the deposited bulk FPKM values (n=2 per group) after log2(FPKM+1) transformation and used the acute-infection contrast for the main model. Standard errors were taken directly from these fitted models. The primary metafor model used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with explicit standard-error input (rma(yi=yi, sei=sei, method="REML")) across all nine eligible independent datasets, including the donor-paired human organoid dataset GSE201465, GSE97036 and GSE262433 at the persistent endpoint. GSE287469 M- and PMN-MDSC effects were collapsed by inverse-variance weighting. Sensitivity analyses used Hartung–Knapp inference, exclusion of persistent infection, substitution of the GSE262433 24-h endpoint, a ten-effect specification retaining both MDSC cell types, exclusion of both reanalysed SRP-linked datasets, and leave-one-dataset-out models. I², τ² and the 95% prediction interval were reported. Egger regression was not used for inference because fewer than ten studies were available. Species and tissue strata were descriptive.

The parallel PRR/adaptor panel comprised TLR2/4/5/6/9, NOD1/2, NLRC4, NLRP3, MYD88, TIRAP and IRAK1/4, defined from canonical bacterial pattern-recognition and proximal signalling components represented in the datasets (21). Each gene used the same dataset-level collapse and REML estimator as the primary TLR5 model. P values were corrected across 13 genes by Benjamini–Hochberg; panel significance refers to q<0.05.

2.7 Statistical software and reproducibility

Analyses used R 4.5.1 with DESeq2 1.48.2, glmmTMB 1.1.14, emmeans 2.0.3, lme4 2.0.1, GEOquery 2.76.0, limma 3.64.3, metafor 5.0.1, ggplot2 4.0.3, patchwork 1.3.2, ggsignif 0.6.4 and ggrepel 0.9.6; Python analyses used Python 3.13 (numpy, pandas, scipy, scanpy, anndata and statsmodels). Seeds were 42 for permutations and 101–103 for hard-negative benchmarks. The versioned analysis archive contains the scripts, processed inputs and model outputs needed to reproduce the reported analyses; source-database access dates are recorded in the release manifest.

## RESULTS

The Results first evaluate bias-controlled PA-target enrichment and then assess whether that network signal is represented in host expression data. Subsequent sections quantify the pooled TLR5 response and its heterogeneity, identify conditions that reverse its direction, and describe the resulting resource outputs. Table 2 lists the strength and the boundary condition of each claim.

3.1 PA-target enrichment depends on curation-source composition

**The curated interactome and its coverage screen.**

We first assembled the PA-human evidence base. We curated PAO1–human records from a 2025 published PA-human interaction resource (4), PHI-base v5.6 (10), IntAct (5), HPIDB (11) and literature on established effectors or adhesins (12, 13), with UniProt/FASTA validation on both sides. The resulting set contained 132 interaction or association pairs (35 PA proteins × 92 human proteins; Fig 1B) (Supplementary Table S1). Evidence was classified as physical interaction (49 pairs; 34 host proteins) or functional/database association; these classes were not treated as equivalent mechanistic evidence. From the full IntAct release we extracted 10,491 curated bacteria–human pairs spanning 102 taxa (Fig 1C; PA excluded; full table in the raw-data release), of which 9,562 had sequences for both partners and were used in the embedding benchmarks.

We trained an ESM2-650M (Evolutionary Scale Modeling 2) embedding classifier (14) as a candidate coverage screen (Supplementary Tables S2–S4). Protein-disjoint five-fold cross-validation gave an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.744; transfer to the PA test set gave 0.740 (bootstrap 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.636–0.841); leave-one-pathogen-out transfer was stable (0.738–0.755; Fig 2C). Against hard negatives the apparent discrimination collapsed: AUC 0.777 against random negatives, 0.413 against the top-300 high-degree host proteins without PA annotation, 0.247 against the highest-degree host-negative stratum, and 0.623 against human proteins known to bind other bacteria (Fig 2D, 2E; Supplementary Table S3). AUC declined with increasing negative-pool host degree (Spearman rho=−0.976). The embedding layer provides genome-wide candidate coverage; its output does not separate interaction specificity from partner-likeness, and the biological claims below rest on the curated interactome and the infection-response layers.

**Enrichment among cross-pathogen-degree hubs and its dependence on the dominant curation source.**

Having defined the evidence base, we next tested whether PA targets were enriched among cross-pathogen hubs. Counting distinct curated bacterial taxa per human protein yielded the expected hub list (FN1 10 species, CDC42 9, RAC1 9, RHOA 8, PLG 8, TP53 7 and CFH 6). In this simple bipartite graph, the number of co-targeting species is mathematically identical to human-node degree, so the value for an individual protein cannot by itself distinguish biological convergence from detection intensity; counting species coverage from an independent interaction resource reproduced the same hub ordering (Fig 2B).

The testable quantity was enrichment of PA targets among high-degree nodes. Among the 61 PA targets represented in the cross-species graph, 28 occurred in the two highest preassigned degree deciles, compared with 12 expected (Fisher odds ratio [OR] 3.47, p=3.8×10⁻⁶); mean degree was 2.85 for PA targets and 1.48 for other proteins (Mann–Whitney p=6.1×10⁻¹³; Fig 2A; Supplementary Table S5). In 10,000 permutations matching the PA-target distribution across interaction-count deciles, the observed mean remained above the null distribution (null mean 1.97; empirical p=1.0×10⁻⁴ with plus-one correction). Restriction to physical PA interactions also retained enrichment (12/24 represented targets in the top deciles; OR 4.04, p=9.4×10⁻⁴). Source sensitivity was asymmetric: exclusion of the literature, IntAct or PHI-base retained ORs of 3.43–3.60, whereas removing the dominant curation source left only nine targets and no clear enrichment (OR 2.67, p=0.263). Source dependence is therefore a measured property of this network rather than a caveat attached to it.

In the 461 proteins with complete cached publication-count and sequence-length covariates, multivariable logistic regression associated cross-species degree with PA-target annotation (OR 1.79 per species, 95% CI 1.35–2.36, p=4.2×10⁻⁵) after adjustment for log10(1+PubMed count) and log2 protein length. Publication count was also associated with PA-target annotation (OR 4.08 per log10 unit), whereas protein length was not (p=0.23); exclusion of the fixed receptor/adaptor panel gave a similar degree association (OR 1.88, p=1.7×10⁻⁵) (Supplementary Table S17). We used a single graph denominator: the 132 pairs contained 92 unique human proteins; 61 occurred in the non-PA cross-species graph and 31 did not. Among the 61 represented proteins, 54 had 1-5 non-PA species and seven had at least six. Absence from the non-PA graph is not biological PA exclusivity; TLR5 was absent from these records despite recognition of conserved bacterial flagellin (22).

**What this layer yields beyond a literature review.** We audited the published literature for the analyses performed here, to separate what the framework adds from what a review already contains. PubMed returns 33 records combining P. aeruginosa with an interactome and network analysis, but none that applies a bias- or ascertainment-corrected convergence analysis, and none that reports a cross-dataset direction or effect size for TLR5 transcript abundance: across 118 papers matching TLR5 and P. aeruginosa, the 237 sentences mentioning TLR5 describe receptor activation, protein interactions, or expression in unrelated contexts. Absence of a keyword combination is weak evidence on its own, and the same audit shows that the literature already reports context-dependent directions, including increased TLR5 in corneal ulcer tissue and in airway neutrophils in cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis. The increment that a literature review cannot supply is therefore quantitative and specific: source dependence of the network enrichment (OR 3.47 falling to 2.67, p=0.263, when the dominant curation source is removed), sensitivity of the sequence classifier to high-degree negative pools (AUC 0.777 to 0.413), and a pooled effect with its between-dataset heterogeneity. Supplementary Tables S20a and S20b record the query counts and the extracted sentences.

3.2 The network signal is reflected only partly in host expression

**Cross-pathogen conservation in human lung tissue.**

We next asked whether the network-level pattern was reflected in a donor-blocked human lung model. Using five donor-matched human lung tissue explants exposed to PA, influenza A virus (IAV), bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) or medium control (GSE192528; (15)), we fitted a donor-blocked negative-binomial model (DESeq2; design = donor + pathogen) with three prespecified contrasts (PA vs control, PA vs IAV and PA vs BCG) (Supplementary Table S15; Fig 3G). A prespecified directional classification was available for 106 focus genes: 9 responded in the same direction to all three pathogens, 13 shared direction between PA and one other pathogen, and 14 were PA-exclusive by the direction threshold (Fig 2F; Supplementary Table S6). TLR5 decreased directionally but not significantly after genome-wide correction (PA vs control −0.55, standard error [SE] 0.32, p=0.090, adjusted p=0.32; PA vs IAV −0.41, p=0.205; PA vs BCG −0.32, p=0.329). Its PA-preference statistic, S = ΔPA − mean(ΔIAV, ΔBCG), was at the 21.4th percentile among expressed genes (39.2nd percentile among genes with |PA log2FC|≥1). In this dataset the TLR5 decrease was consistent in direction but not PA-selective at the available sample size.

**Dual-evidence targets and donor stability.**

To connect this human comparison with cross-species evidence, we next mapped infection-responsive mouse targets to the human curated set. Mapping the meta-analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of two mouse PA lung-infection studies (4) (1,273 genes, false discovery rate [FDR] <0.05, |log2FC|≥1; Mouse Genome Informatics [MGI] orthology (23)) to the 92 curated targets gave 15 dual-evidence targets (RND1/RND3, CBLB, CAMP, LTF (24), C3/C4A, PLAUR, SFN (25, 26), MAPKAPK2, FOS, IFNG, MYD88, TIRAP, TLR5; Supplementary Table S7). Thirteen of these passed human count-model filtering; TLR5 was the only analyzable target with negative mouse and donor-blocked human estimates, while IFNG and C4A lacked estimable human coefficients (Fig 3F). In human explants the infection program was induced (NFKB2 +1.24, p=0.0065; CXCL8 +3.68, p=0.013; IL1B +4.54, p=0.023; Fig 3C), while TLR5 trended downward in four of five donors (−0.55; n=5). A paired-expression leave-one-donor-out jackknife, based on a separate paired-log-expression estimator (reference −0.65), showed that the negative direction was not driven by one donor (range −0.23 to −1.06; Supplementary Table S8; Fig 3D). CAMP was species-divergent (up in mouse, down in human).

3.3 The TLR5 pooled effect is negative but sensitive to dataset composition

**TLR5 responses across heterogeneous infection contexts.**

We then organized the available TLR5 evidence by biological replication and context before formal pooling. TLR5 direction was summarized across the curated eligible datasets and assigned to evidence tiers (Table 1). Tier 1 comprised biologically replicated lung or airway PA comparisons: reanalysed mouse lung GSE233206/SRP439193, acute infection in GSE192890/SRP353174, mouse lung GSE324794 from a severe-pneumonia acute-kidney-injury model (SP-AKI; (16)), human lung explants GSE192528, the six-donor arsenic-exposed primary HBE dataset GSE97036, and the donor-paired human nasopharyngeal airway organoid dataset (GSE201465; six donors; (38, 39)). Tier 2 comprised replicated non-lung or specialized models: human corneal epithelium GSE334176, mouse myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) GSE287469 (17), and the persistent GSE262433 endpoint. GSE276816 (iPSC-derived macrophages; (18)) and GSE274823 (pooled BALF single-cell data; (19)) lacked biological replication at the analyzed level and were descriptive only. Acute Tier 1 estimates were negative or near-null; the polymorphonuclear (PMN)-MDSC estimate was near zero and the persistent GSE262433 endpoint was positive. Within the fixed receptor/adaptor meta-analysis, TLR5 was the only significantly decreased gene after BH correction (Fig 5B), while NLRP3 was significantly increased but with extreme heterogeneity (I²=99.6%).

**Cross-study meta-analysis and receptor-panel evaluation.**

This tiered assessment motivated a formal cross-study estimate of the average TLR5 response. We fitted a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects meta-analysis across all nine eligible datasets, including GSE201465, GSE97036 and the 120-h GSE262433 persistent endpoint, while collapsing the two GSE287469 MDSC cell types to one dataset-level inverse-variance estimate (Fig 5A; Supplementary Table S13). The pooled estimate was −0.86 (95% CI −1.50 to −0.22; z-test p=0.0086), with I²=96.1% and a 95% prediction interval from −2.81 to 1.09. Hartung–Knapp inference gave a 95% CI from −1.61 to −0.11 (p=0.030). Exclusion of persistent GSE262433 yielded −1.07 (95% CI −1.63 to −0.51); exclusion of GSE97036 yielded −0.82 (95% CI −1.54 to −0.09); substitution of the GSE262433 24-h endpoint yielded −0.85 (95% CI −1.51 to −0.18). Exclusion of both reanalysed SRP-linked datasets yielded −0.57 (95% CI −1.20 to 0.06). The pooled direction is consistently negative in these specifications, but high heterogeneity, a prediction interval crossing zero and loss of significance after SRP exclusion do not support a stable universal attenuation claim.

In a parallel REML analysis of the fixed 13-gene PRR/adaptor panel, with BH correction across genes, the panel was restricted to the seven datasets in which all 13 genes were measurable; GSE201465 and GSE262433 were excluded from this panel because at least one panel member was not quantifiable. TLR5 was the only significant negative effect (−1.25, 95% CI −1.77 to −0.72; I²=91.0%; q=4.0×10⁻⁵). NLRP3 (+2.98, q=0.019), TLR2 (+2.11, q=0.022), NOD2 (+1.43, q=0.043) and MYD88 (+1.17, q=0.040) were significant positive effects, all with very high heterogeneity (I²>99%) (Supplementary Table S14; Fig 5B). The panel therefore supports non-global remodelling but not a consistently induced TLR2/MYD88 subset. The panel was recomputed separately in GSE201465, where TLR2, TLR4 and MYD88 were induced while TLR5 was unchanged at the dataset level (Fig 5E; Supplementary Table S19).

**GSE201465 contains opposite subgroup point estimates but no established disease-state interaction.** The near-zero dataset-level estimate for GSE201465 (−0.016, SE 0.089, p=0.86) summarizes subgroup estimates of +0.155 (SE 0.121, p=0.20) in three donors with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and −0.170 (SE 0.098, p=0.084) in three non-diseased donors. The condition × disease-state interaction was +0.338 (SE 0.197, p=0.086, BH-adjusted p=0.45). With three donors per stratum and no significant interaction after correction, these estimates remain hypothesis-generating and do not establish disease-state-dependent reversal (Fig 5E; Supplementary Tables S21a and S21b).

3.4 The direction of TLR5 change reverses under specific infection contexts

**Flagellin removal does not reproduce the TLR5 response.**

Because a negative TLR5 signal could be related to flagellin exposure, we next examined the available ΔFliC contrast. In ER-Hoxb8 mouse MDSC exposed to PAO1 wild type (WT) or ΔFliC (GSE287469; n=3 per group; (17); Fig 4A), a unified limma-voom model tested WT vs control, ΔFliC vs control and WT vs ΔFliC within each cell type. The model had 12 residual degrees of freedom; TOST equivalence was assessed at ±0.5 and ±1.0 log2FC (Supplementary Table S16; Fig 4F). Monocytic (M)-MDSC point estimates were negative for both WT (−1.41, p=0.25) and ΔFliC (−1.26, p=0.30), but the WT-vs-ΔFliC confidence interval was wide (−2.66 to 2.37). Equivalence was not established at either margin (TOST p=0.381 at ±0.5 and p=0.236 at ±1.0). In PMN-MDSC, ΔFliC produced a larger negative estimate than WT (WT-vs-ΔFliC +3.22, 95% CI 1.23 to 5.22, p=0.004) and equivalence was also not established. MYD88 responded in a cell-type-dependent manner across the same contrasts (Fig 4E). The mutant contrast was cell-type dependent, imprecise in M-MDSC and potentially pleiotropic beyond ligand removal; these data do not establish flagellin independence or a single causal role for flagellin.

**Persistent-infection dynamics (GSE262433 dual RNA-seq).**

We then tested whether the direction was maintained in a persistent airway-infection model with matched host and bacterial measurements. We reanalyzed raw host and bacterial counts from tobramycin-maintained Calu-3 air–liquid-interface cultures infected with PAO1 (GSE262433; (20)) using separate DESeq2 models rather than raw-count ratios. Host sample sizes were control n=2 and PA n=3 at 24 h, and n=3 per group at 120 h. At 120 h, 932 host genes met BH q<0.05 and |log2FC|≥1. TLR5 was induced at both time points (+0.90, q=0.0012 at 24 h; +0.73, q=0.0023 at 120 h), and inclusion of the 120-h endpoint changed the all-dataset pooled estimate from −1.07 when persistent infection was excluded to −0.86. C3 (+2.53 then +1.94), MUC1 (+1.10 then +1.43) and RND1 (+1.89 then +1.40) were persistently induced; CFH (−0.58, q=0.046), ITGB1 (−0.80, q=0.014) and EZR (−0.88, q=0.00073) decreased at 120 h. VWF showed a large descriptive switch (+1.30 to −3.22), but DESeq2 flagged the gene as an outlier and did not assign an adjusted p value (Supplementary Table S12).

Within the normalized bacterial transcriptome, fliC decreased from 24 to 120 h (log2FC −0.76, q=0.0043), whereas exoS increased (+2.02, q=0.0068). In this model fliC declined while TLR5 and MUC1 both increased. The sign of the TLR5 response therefore depends on infection model, time and bacterial state, and this reversal is not captured by a single pooled estimate.

3.5 TLR5 and MUC1 are transcriptionally uncoupled across infection states

The six-donor primary HBE dataset provided a repeated-measures test of whether TLR5 attenuation also implied loss of the broader inflammatory response. VST differences were used only as a descriptive sensitivity analysis: TLR5 decreased in all 24 within-donor arsenic-dose comparisons (median VST difference −0.819; range −1.268 to −0.198), whereas MUC1 decreased in 21 of 24 comparisons. For the Meta-analysis, log2 fold changes calculated from size-factor-normalized raw counts were averaged across the four arsenic levels within each donor, so that the six donors rather than the 24 repeated pairs were the independent units. The donor-level normalized-count TLR5 log2FC was −1.224 (95% CI −1.602 to −0.847; t₅=−8.33, p=4.06×10⁻⁴), and all six donor means were negative. MUC1 decreased by −0.195 log2 units (95% CI −0.370 to −0.019; t₅=−2.86, p=0.036). The donor-level TLR5-minus-MUC1 difference was −1.029 log2 units (95% CI −1.447 to −0.612; p=0.00144). NFKB1, NFKB2, RELA, IL6 and CXCL8 increased in all six donor summaries (Fig 6A). Donor mean TLR5 change was not correlated with MUC1 change (Spearman rho=−0.314, p=0.544) or with the inflammatory transcript axis (rho=−0.257, p=0.623). The random-intercept model yielded a log2-scaled average TLR5 effect of −1.232 (SE 0.068) and MUC1 effect of −0.189 (0.042); the donor-fixed sensitivity model gave −1.230 (0.063) and −0.189 (0.039), respectively. The arsenic × infection likelihood-ratio tests were not significant for TLR5 (p=0.952) or MUC1 (p=0.597). The cross-study meta-analysis uses the six-donor normalized-count TLR5 log2FC (−1.224, SE 0.147); VST values are not used as Meta-analysis inputs. This dataset therefore separates receptor-transcript attenuation from inactivity of the downstream inflammatory transcriptional response.

The direction of the two transcripts was not fixed across models. In persistent PAO1 infection of Calu-3 cultures, both TLR5 and MUC1 increased at 24 and 120 h while bacterial fliC declined. In iPSC-derived macrophages, by contrast, TLR5 decreased over the time course while MUC1 increased (Fig 3E and Fig 6B). These observations define acute primary-epithelial, persistent-epithelial and myeloid states with different TLR5/MUC1 configurations. Because the comparisons span different platforms, species and infection models, and because RNA abundance does not determine surface protein or signalling capacity, this context pattern is hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a causal MUC1–TLR5 circuit.

3.6 PA-HIR returns separate interaction and expression evidence layers

**Resource outputs and intended use.**

PA-HIR is designed to return evidence layers rather than a single therapeutic ranking. For each host protein, the release records interaction evidence and assay class, recurrence across bacterial taxa, source sensitivity of the network enrichment, infection-response estimates by dataset, replication unit, direction and uncertainty. The same record therefore supports a reproducible query such as whether a candidate remains supported after dominant-source removal, whether its apparent interaction breadth tracks promiscuous-hub behaviour, and whether its infection-response direction is stable across contexts. This structure allows users to retain the evidence they need for a biological question without treating a composite score as a target-efficacy estimate.

**Single-cell localization of TLR5.**

Finally, we examined whether the transcriptomic signal could be localized to relevant cell compartments. In GSE274823 (mouse BALF, WT/double knockout [DKO] × sham/PA; (19)), PA and sham libraries differed markedly in cell composition (Fig 4B). Within the WT pooled libraries, Tlr5 mean log-normalized expression and detected fraction were 0.082 and 13.1% in sham alveolar macrophages versus 0.016 and 3.8% in PA alveolar macrophages, and 0.088 and 12.0% versus 0.003 and 0.8% in cDCs (Supplementary Table S11; Fig 4C, 4D). BALF from three mice was pooled into one library per condition, so infection, cell composition and library are not separable. These values are descriptive localization only; cells were not treated as independent biological replicates and no between-group inferential test is reported.

## DISCUSSION

This study shows both the utility and the limits of combining sparse host–pathogen interaction data with infection transcriptomes. Curated PA targets were enriched among cross-pathogen-degree hubs after interaction-count matching, physical-interaction restriction and adjustment for publication count and protein length. Removing the dominant curation source substantially reduced the available PA target set and eliminated clear enrichment, so the network result is source-dependent prioritization. The ESM2 benchmark reaches the same boundary: discrimination against random negatives collapsed from AUC 0.78 to 0.41 against top-300 hub negatives and 0.25 against the highest-degree hub pool. Network and embedding layers therefore provide candidate coverage rather than interaction specificity.

The revised TLR5 meta-analysis did not establish a stable universal attenuation effect. Across all nine eligible datasets, the pooled estimate was −0.86 (95% CI −1.50 to −0.22), but heterogeneity was 96.1% and the prediction interval crossed zero. Exclusion of both reanalysed SRP-linked datasets attenuated the estimate to −0.57 (95% CI −1.20 to 0.06). The appropriate conclusion is that negative estimates predominate in several acute contexts but that the pooled direction is sensitive to dataset composition and model context.

The primary HBE analysis provides a more specific result. TLR5 transcript decreased in all 24 infected-versus-control comparisons across six donors and four arsenic levels, and the six donor-level means were all negative. NF-κB-associated and inflammatory-response transcripts increased, while donor mean TLR5 change was not correlated with the inflammatory-transcript axis. This separates receptor-transcript attenuation from inactivation of the broader inflammatory transcriptional response. Across contexts, however, transcripts encoding the previously linked proteins TLR5 and MUC1 did not form a fixed module: both decreased in several acute epithelial settings, both increased in persistent Calu-3 infection, and MUC1 increased while TLR5 decreased in iPSC macrophages (37). These patterns support a context-dependent transcriptional decoupling hypothesis, not a new causal MUC1–TLR5 mechanism.

TLR5 is a biologically plausible candidate because flagellin–TLR5 signalling contributes to airway recognition and protection in PA infection (22, 27, 28), and the receptor–ligand interface is structurally defined (30). These data constrain, but do not identify, the mechanism of the transcript change. ΔFliC contrasts were cell-type dependent and did not establish equivalence, while the normalized persistent-infection analysis showed TLR5 and MUC1 induction together with reduced fliC expression at 120 h. Pathogen-side variation is plausible: PA can evade mammalian flagellin recognition, motility can affect lung disease separately from FliC ligation, and flagellin sequence can alter TLR5 agonism in other Gram-negative pathogens (31, 32, 33). Pooled single-cell libraries provided descriptive localization of lower Tlr5 detection to macrophage and dendritic-cell compartments, with infection and library not separable. Competing explanations therefore include cell composition, host negative feedback, pathogen-state differences and model-specific stress responses. Transcript abundance also constrains rather than determines total protein, surface receptor availability and flagellin responsiveness; direct measurements of TLR5, MUC1 and flagellin responsiveness are required to test the context-dependent transcriptional-decoupling hypothesis.

The resource implication is that interaction support and infection-response direction should be reported as separate fields. Existing flagellin/TLR5 studies support the biological relevance of the receptor (27, 28, 29), but they do not remove the context dependence observed here. A user of PA-HIR can therefore retrieve both the interaction evidence and the conflicting infection-response layers, then decide which experimental context is appropriate. This preserves the known biology while making the uncertainty and the conditions requiring a new experiment explicit.

Several limitations determine the evidential scope. Public datasets were assembled as a curated, non-systematic set rather than through a registered systematic review, so selection bias cannot be excluded. No verifiable pre-result record supported excluding persistent GSE262433; the revised primary model therefore included it and reported exclusion as a sensitivity analysis. The human and mouse contexts differ in tissue, cell composition, infection stage and platform, and TLR5 heterogeneity remained high after correction. GSE201465 had opposite subgroup point estimates (+0.155 in COPD donors and −0.170 in non-diseased donors), but the interaction was not significant after correction (q=0.45); it is therefore not evidence of established disease-state reversal. GSE287469 contributed correlated cell-type effects that required dataset-level collapse. GSE276816 and GSE274823 lacked independent biological replication at the analyzed level. The network set was small and dominated by one source, and the regression used a covariate-complete subset. Finally, the context-dependent TLR5/MUC1 transcript pattern is RNA-level and cross-model; it does not establish protein abundance, surface localization or functional coupling. PA-HIR reports these structures explicitly so that users can select and combine only the evidence layers appropriate to their question.
